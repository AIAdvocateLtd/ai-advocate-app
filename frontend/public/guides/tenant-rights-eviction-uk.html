Tenant rights and eviction notices: can my landlord evict me?

Getting an eviction notice is frightening, but it doesn't mean you have to leave straight away. Here's what your landlord must do, and what you can do.

Last reviewed: October 2026 · Covers England, Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland
Quick answer: in almost all cases your landlord must give you the correct written notice and then get a court order (a tribunal order in Scotland) before you can be evicted. Changing the locks, cutting off utilities or removing your things without one is usually a criminal offence. Get free help from Shelter or Citizens Advice as soon as you receive a notice.

England: the Renters' Rights Act changes

The Renters' Rights Act 2025 has changed private renting in England, with the main changes from 1 May 2026:

  • Section 21 "no-fault" evictions are abolished. Landlords must use a legal reason (a "ground") under section 8, such as rent arrears, serious anti-social behaviour, or wanting to sell or move in.
  • Most tenancies become rolling (periodic) tenancies, and you can usually leave by giving 2 months' notice.
  • Rent increases are limited to once a year using a formal notice, and you can challenge unfair increases at the First-tier Tribunal.

How the transition applies depends on when your notice was served, so check GOV.UK or get advice.

Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland

  • Wales: under the Renting Homes (Wales) Act 2016 you're a "contract-holder". A no-fault notice usually has to give at least 6 months, and can't normally be given in the first 6 months of the contract.
  • Scotland: most private tenants have a private residential tenancy. Landlords must use one of the legal eviction grounds and apply to the First-tier Tribunal (Housing and Property Chamber).
  • Northern Ireland: landlords must give a written notice to quit with the correct notice period (which depends on how long you've lived there) and get a court order.

Illegal eviction and harassment

Under the Protection from Eviction Act 1977 (and similar laws in Scotland and NI), it's usually a criminal offence for a landlord to force you out without a court order or to harass you into leaving. This includes changing the locks, removing your belongings, cutting off gas, electricity or water, or repeated threats. If it's happening now, call the police and contact your council's housing team.

What to do if you get a notice

  1. Don't ignore it, and don't move out yet. A notice is not an order to leave.
  2. Check the date, type of notice and the reason given. Notices with mistakes may be invalid.
  3. Check your deposit was protected and that you were given required documents — problems here can affect the landlord's case.
  4. Contact your council if you might become homeless; it has duties to help you, often up to 56 days before.
  5. Reply to any court papers by the deadline, and go to the hearing.

Repairs: your right to a safe home

Your landlord must keep the structure, heating, hot water, gas, electrics and sanitation in good repair, and the home must be fit to live in (Homes (Fitness for Human Habitation) Act 2018 in England). Report repairs in writing and keep copies and photos. If nothing happens, contact your council's environmental health team.

Frequently asked questions

Can my landlord evict me without a court order?

No, in almost all cases. Only court bailiffs (or sheriff officers in Scotland) can evict you, after a court or tribunal order.

Has section 21 been abolished?

In England, yes — the Renters' Rights Act 2025 abolishes section 21, with the main changes from 1 May 2026. Check how the transition affects your tenancy.

Do I have to leave when the notice ends?

Not usually. The landlord then has to go to court, and you can defend the claim. Get advice straight away.

Can my landlord enter without permission?

Generally no. Landlords should give at least 24 hours' written notice for visits, except in a genuine emergency.
